# Q2 — composition notes (private)

Working notes on the Greek of `q2-obedience-god-and-laws.md`, construction by
construction, in the order the essay uses them. Section references (§) point
to `docs/greek-prose-composition.md`. Not for the public repo.

## The opening question

**πότερον τὸ τῷ θεῷ πειθαρχεῖν σύμφωνόν ἐστι τῷ τοῖς νόμοις πειθαρχεῖν;**

- *πότερον* opens a direct alternative question; here the second limb ("or
  not") is left implicit, which is idiomatic. In indirect form the same
  πότερον... ἤ frame carries "whether... or" (§8).
- *τὸ... πειθαρχεῖν*, twice: the articular infinitive (§3) turns each act of
  obedience into a noun that can be subject and complement of the same
  sentence. This is the essay's central device — the whole question is posed
  as a relation between two nominalized acts.
- Each articular infinitive sandwiches its own dative complement (τῷ θεῷ, τοῖς
  νόμοις) between article and infinitive — the same tight "sandwich" order as
  the attributive genitive (§2). πειθαρχέω takes the dative.
- *σύμφωνον* + dative: "in accord *with*."

## First paragraph — the thesis and the apparent conflict

**περὶ τῆς τοῦ Σωκράτους πράξεως** — a fronted περί-phrase announces the topic
before the syntax proper begins (§2), and inside it sits a sandwich genitive:
τῆς **τοῦ Σωκράτους** πράξεως.

**δοκεῖ μοι συμφωνεῖν ἀλλήλοιν τὼ τοῦ Πλάτωνος διαλόγω** — impersonal δοκεῖ +
accusative-infinitive (§6). The subject of the infinitive, τὼ διαλόγω, is
dual — as is ἀλλήλοιν, "with each other." Note that δοκεῖ stays singular: with
a personal construction ("the two dialogues seem...") the verb would have to
agree with the dual/plural subject, so the singular is what forces the
impersonal, accusative-infinitive parse on the reader.

**εἰ καὶ δόξειεν ἄν τινι διαφωνεῖν** — two things at once:

- *εἰ καί* = "even if," conceding a fact-shaped objection (§7); distinguish
  καὶ εἰ, "even supposing."
- *δόξειεν ἄν* is the potential optative (§6) in the **aorist**: the aspect
  makes it a momentary flicker of impression — "it might strike someone."
  δοκοίη ἄν (present) would be a standing, continuing view. Aspect before
  tense (§4), applied inside the moods.

**ἐναντία ἑαυτῷ φαίνοιτ᾿ ἂν λέγειν καὶ πράττειν** — φαίνομαι discriminates by
its complement (§5): with the **infinitive**, as here, it means "would *seem*
to say and do contrary things (but perhaps doesn't)" — exactly the reservation
the essay needs, since the next two paragraphs dissolve the appearance. With a
participle (φαίνοιτ᾿ ἂν λέγων) it would concede "is manifestly contradicting
himself," and the essay would have refuted itself in its own statement of the
problem.

**ἐν μὲν τῇ «Ἀπολογίᾳ»..., ἐν δὲ τῷ «Κρίτωνι»...** — textbook μέν/δέ (§1):
the two units are exactly parallel (two prepositional phrases hanging off the
same construction), balanced in sense (two dialogues, two behaviors), and μέν
and δέ stand immediately after the contrasted words — the dialogue titles.

**ὑποθέμενος ὑπόθεσιν ἐν ᾗ...** — the participle's subject is Socrates, who is
also the subject of the main verb φησί, so this is the plain nominative
circumstantial participle, *not* a genitive absolute (§5) — the absolute is
only for a subject that has no role in the main clause. ὑποθέμενος ὑπόθεσιν is
a cognate accusative, a figure Plato himself likes.

**οἱ δικασταὶ ἀπολύουσιν αὑτὸν τῆς αἰτίας** — *αὑτόν* is the **indirect
reflexive** (§10): inside the hypothesis Socrates is entertaining, the
reflexive reaches back to Socrates, the thinker whose thought this is — "they
acquit *him*," not "themselves." The logophoric pattern (Latin sē). A plain
αὐτόν would merely say "him" with no signal that the frame is his own thought.
ἀπολύω τινά τινος: genitive of the charge.

**ἐφ᾿ ᾧτε μηκέτι φιλοσοφεῖν** — "on condition that" + infinitive (§12). The
negative is μηκέτι, not οὐκέτι, because a condition is non-factual and
infinitives outside indirect statement take μή (§9).

**«πείσομαι», φησί, «μᾶλλον τῷ θεῷ ἢ ὑμῖν» (29d)** — quotation mechanics
(§8): postpositive φησί inserted after the first word of the quotation. Plato's
own text runs πείσομαι **δὲ** μᾶλλον τῷ θεῷ — that δέ was the seam joining the
sentence to *its* context, not yours, so trimming it is correct practice, not
misquotation.

## Second paragraph — first argument

**πῶς οὖν συμφωνεῖ ταῦτα;** — οὖν resumes the argument after the exposition
(§1). ταῦτα is neuter plural, so the verb is singular — the τὰ ζῷα τρέχει
rule.

**πρῶτον μὲν γὰρ** — the double particle shows the division of labor (§1):
μέν only points forward (to the further arguments coming), so it cannot join
this sentence to the last; γάρ does the actual backward connecting. A sentence
may open with μέν only if some other word carries the connection.

**οὐδεὶς ἦν νόμος ὁ κωλύων φιλοσοφεῖν** — article + participle as a relative
clause (§3): "no law existed — the one forbidding philosophy," i.e. "no law
that forbade it." The appositional article-phrase after an anarthrous noun is
the classical way to add a defining relative to an indefinite head.

**εἰ οἱ δικασταὶ ἀφίοιεν... παράνομος ἂν εἴη αὕτη ἡ δίκη** — future less
vivid, the grid's argumentative workhorse (§7): optative in both halves, ἄν in
the apodosis only. Note also αὕτη ἡ δίκη — demonstrative in predicate
position (§2).

**ἐξετάζοντα ἑαυτόν τε καὶ τοὺς ἄλλους** — τε...καί "both...and," with τε
after the first word of the first member (§1). ἑαυτόν is the plain
direct reflexive this time — same clause, same subject. Accent: ἑαυτόν keeps
its acute before the enclitic τε where it would take the grave before an
ordinary word (§12).

## Third paragraph — objection and reply

**ἀλλὰ φαίη ἄν τις ὅτι...** — the stock hypophora formula for an objection
(§6). One open question for revision: classical φημί strongly prefers the
accusative-infinitive over ὅτι (§8). εἴποι ἄν τις ὅτι would keep the formula
and the finite clause; or keep φαίη and convert to accusative-infinitive.
Propose-only — your call.

**ἐπεὶ εἰκόνες τῶν νόμων εἰσίν** — causal ἐπεί; εἰκόνες is anarthrous because
it is predicate (§3). Accent: paroxytone εἰκόνες + disyllabic enclitic εἰσίν
leaves the enclitic its own accent (§12).

**ταὐτόν ἐστι τὸ τοῖς δικασταῖς ἀπειθεῖν καὶ τὸ τοῖς νόμοις** — "the same
as" by ὁ αὐτὸς καί (§12), with crasis (τὸ αὐτόν → ταὐτόν) and the second
articular infinitive elliptical — τὸ τοῖς νόμοις [ἀπειθεῖν] — a clean
economy once the parallel is set.

**οὐδαμῶς·** — licensed asyndeton (§1): an answer to a question needs no
connective; the abrupt refusal is the rhetoric.

**εἰ γὰρ καὶ... εἰσίν, ἀμυδρῶς ἐμφαίνονται** — γάρ grounds the οὐδαμῶς;
εἰ καί concedes the objection's premise while the main clause takes back its
force. Concession granted, conclusion denied — the παρὰ μὲν... ὅμως δέ move
in miniature.

**ὥσπερ γὰρ οἱ νόμοι..., οὕτω καὶ αἱ δίκαι...** — the correlative frame
ὥσπερ... οὕτω καί, Greek's standard analogy syntax; the argument's engine
(laws : justice :: verdicts : laws) is carried entirely by the frame.

**ἡ δὲ εἰκὼν ἀεί πῃ ἀπολείπεται τοῦ παραδείγματος** — ἀπολείπεται + genitive
of separation/comparison: the image "falls short *of*" its paradigm. Note the
Platonic vocabulary (εἰκών, παράδειγμα) doing the philosophical work.

**ἐνίοτε γὰρ αἱ δίκαι παράνομοί εἰσιν** — a second γάρ grounding the ground
(§1: chains of γάρ are normal). Accent: proparoxytone παράνομοι + enclitic
εἰσιν yields the second acute, παράνομοί εἰσιν (§12).

## Fourth paragraph

**τὸ δὲ μέγιστον·** — "and the greatest point:" — the fronted nominalized
superlative with raised dot licenses the following asyndeton (§1); a high
rhetorical moment marked exactly as the idiom wants.

## Fifth paragraph — the anticipated question and the close

**ἀλλ᾿ ἴσως ἔροιτο ἄν τις· τί ἐστιν αὐτὸ τὸ δίκαιον;** — the second hypophora
formula, the question-form partner of φαίη ἄν τις (§6). αὐτὸ τὸ δίκαιον —
αὐτός in predicate position, "justice *itself*," the Platonic technical
intensive.

**οὐ νῦν σκεπτέον...** — verbal adjective in -τέος (§6): "it need not now be
examined." Negated with οὐ because it is a plain factual assertion about the
present argument's needs, not a prohibition.

**τὸ τί ἦν εἶναι τῆς δικαιοσύνης** — Aristotle's term of art for essence,
article-nominalized whole. Post-classical/technical by Attic standards — in
Dickey's terms a vocabulary flaw, in this essay a deliberate wink; know which
dial you are turning (§11).

**δεῖ... δεῖ... δεῖ...** — impersonal δεῖ + infinitive three times over:
anaphora with rhetorical asyndeton inside the tricolon; the list-form itself
is the "licensed" case of omitted connection (§1).

**ὃν οὐ λόγῳ μόνον ἀλλὰ καὶ ἔργῳ ἐβεβαίωσεν** — relative clause with the
οὐ μόνον... ἀλλὰ καί frame; λόγῳ / ἔργῳ datives of means, and the λόγος/ἔργον
antithesis is itself the most Attic of clichés — earned here, since the
essay's point is that Socrates confirmed his word by deed.

**θάνατον πρὸ τῆς φυγῆς εἵλετο** — middle αἱρέομαι, "chose for himself";
πρό + genitive, "in preference to." Accent and breathing on the second vowel
of the diphthong: εἵλετο (§12).

**οἱ δὲ ἔσχατοι τοῦ «Κρίτωνος» λόγοι οἵδε·** — forward-pointing ὅδε with the
raised dot formally introduces the quotation (§10, §8): "are these:".

**πράττωμεν ταύτῃ** (in the Plato quote) — hortatory subjunctive (§6);
worth noticing that the essay's final authority arrives in the mood of shared
resolve.

**σύμφωνον ἄρα τὸ τῷ θεῷ πειθαρχεῖν τῷ τοῖς νόμοις πειθαρχεῖν.** — ἄρα draws
the conclusion (§1), and the sentence is the opening question turned into a
statement — same articular infinitives, same dative, question mark traded for
the particle of QED. Ring composition at the level of syntax.

## From the drafting (not in the final text)

- **ἃ ἂν κελεύωσιν** — indefinite relative, ὃς ἄν + subjunctive: "whatever
  they command" (§7). Dropped from the final version but worth keeping in the
  active repertoire; it is the conditional grid wearing relative clothes.
- **τιμᾶσθαι + genitive (ἧς)** — what looked like relative attraction was the
  verb's own case-frame: τιμᾶσθαι "to propose as penalty" governs the genitive
  of the penalty, so ἧς was the true case, not an attracted one. The moral is
  the checking order (§10): verb's case-frame first, attraction second.
- **The Woodhouse "manifest" episode** — an English–Greek dictionary offered a
  word; LSJ showed its actual usage didn't fit. The iron rule of §11, learned
  in the field: Woodhouse proposes, LSJ disposes.
