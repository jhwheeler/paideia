In Iliad Book VI, after Hector's wife Andromache exhorts him to refrain from re-entering the battle, she ends her appeal with something quite odd for a woman of the era -- a battle strategy suggestion:

λαὸν δὲ στῆσον παρ’ ἑρινεόν, ἔνθα μάλιστα
ἀμβατός ἐστι πόλις καὶ ἐπίδρομον ἔπλετο τεῖχος.
τρὶς γὰρ τῇ γ’ ἐλθόντες ἐπειρήσανθ’ οἱ ἄριστοι
ἀμφ’ Αἴαντε δύω καὶ ἀγακλυτὸν Ἰδομενῆα
ἠδ’ ἀμφ’ Ἀτρεῗδας καὶ Τυδέος ἄλκιμον υἱόν·
ἤ πού τίς σφιν ἔνισπε θεοπροπίων ἐὑ εἰδώς,
ἤ νυ καὶ αὐτῶν θυμὸς ἐποτρύνει καὶ ἀνώγει.

- Iliad VI.433-439

Reading this, I thought, "Why a fig? And why not συκῆ, the usual word for fig?" Researching the matter, it turns out that ἐρινεός is the male fig, and its use here as the marker of the weak point in the wall is poignant. Not only does the male fig obviously not bear fruit, fig wood in general is weak and spongy, making for poor timber and mediocre firewood.

Whether or not the poet was intentionally creating this symbolism, the image of the weak wood of the fig standing at the weak point of the walls of the otherwise impenetrable Troy is a striking one. One alternative explanation is that this image was placed there subconsciously, but correctly, because the poet was living in a time when people had more intimate acquaintance with nature and thus the properties and qualities of various plants and animals. It is safe to assume that fig's lack of utility as timber or firewood was common knowledge in Homer's Greece. Thus the image could have come quite naturally to the poet, even if it were not an intentional symbol.

Another interesting connection can be made with the preceding point in the narrative. Hector is meeting his wife on the walls of Troy, coming directly from his brother Paris's chambers, where he was scolding Paris for his weakness, cowardice, and absence from the battlefield. For not only was Paris's transgression of stealing away Helen from Sparta the spark that ignited the war to begin with, earlier that very day Paris and Menelaus had entered into a duel that would have decided the war: Priam and Agamemnon took an oath that whoever won the duel would take Helen and her treasures, and the Greeks would leave the shores of Troy for good. However, just when Menelaus was about to defeat Paris, his patron goddess Aphrodite whisked him away from the battlefield and into the safety of his palace. Shortly thereafter, the Lycian prince Pandaros, one of the Trojan allies, was tricked by Athena (who yearned for the destruction of Troy) into breaking the sacred oath by shooting an arrow at Menelaos...and the battle begins. Since no one knows that Aphrodite saved Paris, they all think that he absconded from the duel due to cowardice and fear of death. Andromache referring to the weak male fig tree at the weak point of Troy's walls harken back to the preceding scene, where Hector scolds his brother for weakness and cowardice. Paris, the weak point of the Trojan forces, represented by the male fig tree at the weak point in their otherwise impenetrable walls.
