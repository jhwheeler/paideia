In Iliad Book VI, after Hector's wife Andromache exhorts him to refrain from re-entering the battle, she ends her appeal with something quite odd for a woman of the era -- a battle strategy suggestion:

λαὸν δὲ στῆσον παρ’ ἑρινεόν, ἔνθα μάλιστα
ἀμβατός ἐστι πόλις καὶ ἐπίδρομον ἔπλετο τεῖχος.
τρὶς γὰρ τῇ γ’ ἐλθόντες ἐπειρήσανθ’ οἱ ἄριστοι
ἀμφ’ Αἴαντε δύω καὶ ἀγακλυτὸν Ἰδομενῆα
ἠδ’ ἀμφ’ Ἀτρεῗδας καὶ Τυδέος ἄλκιμον υἱόν·
ἤ πού τίς σφιν ἔνισπε θεοπροπίων ἐῢ εἰδώς,
ἤ νυ καὶ αὐτῶν θυμὸς ἐποτρύνει καὶ ἀνώγει.

And for thy host, stay it by the wild fig-tree,
where the city may best be scaled, and the wall is open to assault.
For thrice at this point came the most valiant
in company with the twain Aiantes and glorious Idomeneus
and the sons of Atreus and the valiant son of Tydeus, and made essay to enter:
whether it be that one well-skilled in soothsaying told them,
or haply their own spirit urgeth and biddeth them thereto.

- Iliad VI.433-439 (A. Murray translation)

Reading this, I thought, "Why a fig? And why not συκῆ, the usual word for fig?" Researching the matter, it turns out that ἐρινεός is the male fig, and its use here as the marker of the weak point in the wall is poignant. Not only does the male fig not bear edible fruit, fig wood in general is weak and spongy, making for poor timber and mediocre firewood.

Whether or not the poet was intentionally creating this symbolism, the image of the weak wood of the fig standing at the weak point of the walls of the otherwise impenetrable Troy is a striking one. Perhaps this image was placed there subconsciously, but correctly, because the poet was living in a time when people had more intimate acquaintance with nature and thus the properties and qualities of various plants and animals. It is safe to assume that fig's lack of utility as timber or firewood was common knowledge in Homer's Greece. Thus the image could have come quite naturally to the poet, even if it were not an intentional symbol.

But why should the supposedly impenetrable walls of Troy have a weak point at all? Pindar's eighth Olympian ode (vv. 31–46) gives the answer. When Apollo and Poseidon set out to build the wall, they summoned a mortal co-worker, Aeacus. Destiny required that Troy be able to fall, and a wall built wholly by the gods could never be breached. As soon as the work was finished, an omen appeared: serpents assaulted the rampart. While two fell back, one forced its way in right where the wall was built by mortal hands. Apollo read the sign and prophesied that Troy would be taken where Aeacus had worked.

You might say that the prophecy never came true, since the Greeks defeated the Trojans with Odysseus's famous trick of the Trojan horse. However, the prophecy actually did come true, just not in the war we usually think of, for in fact, Troy was sacked twice. One generation before the Trojan War, Heracles took the city after King Laomedon, Priam's father, had cheated Apollo and Poseidon of their promised wage for the building work. Fighting beside Heracles was Telamon, Aeacus's own son, and the tradition Pindar draws on had Telamon breach the wall first, exactly at his father's weak stretch.

Another interesting connection can be made with the preceding point in the narrative. Hector is meeting his wife on the walls of Troy, coming directly from his brother Paris's chambers, where he was scolding Paris for his weakness, cowardice, and absence from the battlefield. For not only was Paris's transgression of stealing away Helen from Sparta the spark that ignited the war to begin with, earlier that very day Paris and Menelaus had entered into a duel that would have decided the war: Priam and Agamemnon took an oath that whoever won the duel would take Helen and her treasures, and the Greeks would leave the shores of Troy for good. Menelaus was on the verge of victory when Paris's patron goddess Aphrodite whisked him from the field and set him down in the safety of his own bedchamber to make love to his wife Helen. Shortly thereafter, the Trojan prince Pandarus was tricked by Athena, yearning for the destruction of Troy, into breaking the sacred oath by shooting an arrow at Menelaus...and the battle begins in earnest. Since no one knows that Aphrodite saved Paris, they all think that he absconded from the duel due to cowardice and fear of death.

Andromache referring to the weak male fig tree at the weak point of Troy's walls hearkens back to the preceding scene, where Hector scolds his brother for weakness and cowardice. Paris, the weak point of the Trojan forces, represented by the beautiful, yet fruitless, male fig tree at the weak point in their otherwise impenetrable walls.

Now, the symbolism takes on a fresh meaning in the history of the literary criticism of the text. Aristarchus of Samothrace (c. 216 – c. 144 BC), one of the most influential scholars of Homeric poetry in ancient times, rejects exactly these lines as spurious. His reasoning has a few horns: it is out of character for a woman of this era to be giving military advice (especially since her characterization is simply as Hector's wife, not as an advisor); there are no other references in the Iliad to these three attempts to scale the wall at the fig tree; and perhaps most compellingly, Hector doesn't address this suggestion in his reply, making the text flow naturally with these lines removed.

Thus we see that the weak point in the wall, marked by the weak wood of the fruitless male fig, is rejected as a hole in the narrative itself -- resulting in an enactment of the story in its reception, the text's afterlife imitating the text.
