# Greek prose composition — a working manual

A craft manual for composing Attic prose, organized by the decisions you make
while writing: how to join this sentence to the last one, where the verb's
weight falls, which mood the thought requires. It is built on the public-domain
tradition — North & Hillard's *Greek Prose Composition for Schools* (1898) for
graded drill, Auden's *Greek Prose Phrase-Book* (1899) for idiom, and Smyth's
*Greek Grammar* (1920) as the court of appeal — with a handful of attributed
debts to Eleanor Dickey's *An Introduction to the Composition and Analysis of
Greek Prose* (Cambridge, 2016), which is warmly recommended as the modern
course. Almost every rule below has exceptions; the references exist so you can
find them.

Each section ends with pointers: **Drill** names North & Hillard (N&H) pages
whose exercises practice the construction; **Lookup** names the fuller
treatment.

---

## 1. The skeleton — verbs and connectives

The deepest structural fact about Greek prose, in Dickey's formulation: "A
Greek sentence has a skeleton composed of verbs and connectives; most
sentences, and indeed paragraphs, can be analyzed solely on the basis of such
words." Everything else — nouns, adjectives, adverbs — is muscle hung on that
skeleton. When you compose, build the skeleton first; when you revise, audit it
first.

- **One verb-form per unit.** Every infinitive and participle opens its own
  unit of thought (attributive participles like ὁ κωλύων νόμος do not — they
  behave as adjectives). Each unit is either *coordinated* with its neighbor,
  which requires a conjunction, or *subordinated* to it — by conjunction,
  relative, participle, or infinitive. There is no third option; two finite
  verbs cannot simply sit side by side.
- **Nearly every sentence connects to its predecessor** with a small word near
  its front: δέ (and, but), καί (and), ἀλλά (but), γάρ (for), οὖν (therefore).
  English happily starts sentences cold; Greek does not, so when composing from
  English thought you must *add* connectives that the English never had. The
  habit runs so deep that Plato connects sentences even across a change of
  speaker in dialogue.
- **Punctuation is irrelevant to connection.** Classical Greek was written
  without it; a full stop between your sentences changes nothing about the need
  for a connective. Ask of every sentence: what word joins this to what came
  before?
- **μέν cannot connect backward.** It only looks forward to a coming δέ. A
  sentence opening with a μέν-phrase therefore still needs its own backward
  link — ἡ μὲν γὰρ βουλή..., where γάρ does the connecting and μέν does the
  forward-pointing.
- **The workhorses.** γάρ grounds what precedes (chains of γάρ are normal:
  state, ground, then ground the ground); οὖν resumes an argument or draws an
  inference; ἄρα marks the conclusion clinched; δέ is the colorless "next."
- **Licensed asyndeton** — connection omitted on purpose: when a sentence
  specifies or unpacks what a raised dot (·) announced; in answers to
  questions; and at moments of rhetorical height, where the abruptness itself
  is the effect.

**Drill:** N&H "Connection," pp. 44–45; particles table, pp. 238–239.
**Lookup:** Smyth on particles; Denniston, *The Greek Particles*, for the full
doctrine.

### μέν... δέ

Harder than it looks. Three conditions, all necessary:

1. The paired units must be **exactly grammatically parallel** — two finite
   clauses, or two participles, but never a participle paired against its own
   main verb, and never a subordinate clause against its superordinate.
2. They must be **balanced in sense**. If the real relation is causal or
   consequential, μέν/δέ misdescribes it — subordinate instead, or use οὖν.
   "The slave was afraid and fled" is a causal chain wearing parallel clothes.
3. μέν and δέ must **immediately follow the specific words being contrasted**:
   ἐν μὲν τῇ Ἀπολογίᾳ..., ἐν δὲ τῷ Κρίτωνι... If the clauses contain no such
   focal words, the idiom is impractical — don't force it.

Related: τε...καί = "both...and," with τε after the first word of the first
member (ὅ τε πρῶτος δοῦλος καὶ ὁ δεύτερος). Bare τε for "and" is poetic;
avoid it in prose.

## 2. Word order — units, openers, the focus slot

Greek word order is free only in the sense that it carries information instead
of syntax. The working model:

- A unit opens with a **distinctive opening word** — conjunction, relative,
  preposition, article — that points forward to its verb; everything between
  opener and verb belongs to the unit.
- **Postpositives stand second**: δέ, γάρ, οὖν, τε, τοι, ἄν, and the
  enclitics. This makes them boundary-markers — the clause began with the word
  *before* the postpositive. They intrude even into otherwise inviolable
  groups: ἡ δὲ βουλή.
- **The slot directly before the verb is the focus slot**, given to the word on
  which the unit's attention falls. Decide what the sentence is *about*, and
  what it *asserts*; the assertion's key word wants that slot.
- **Adverbs and negatives stand directly before what they modify**; an adverb
  modifying the whole sentence goes before the verb (οἴκαδε πάλιν ἥκει, not
  ἥκει οἴκαδε πάλιν).
- **ἄν moves by clause type**: in subordinate clauses it hugs the opening word
  (hence the fused forms ἐάν, ὅταν, ἐπειδάν); in main clauses it stays near
  the verb; if the clause has οὐ, ἄν follows it (οὐκ ἂν εἴη).
- **Attributive vs. predicate position is meaning, not style**: ὁ σοφὸς ἀνήρ
  "the wise man" vs. σοφὸς ὁ ἀνήρ "the man is wise." πᾶς, αὐτός, and the
  demonstratives take predicate position: οὗτος ὁ ἀνήρ, "this man."
- The **sandwich genitive** is the tight, formal order: ἡ τοῦ Σωκράτους
  σοφία. Keep appositives adjacent to their noun; if a sentence's topic needs
  announcing, front it with a περί-phrase and let the syntax restart cleanly
  after.

**Drill:** N&H "Position of the Article," pp. 4–7.
**Lookup:** Smyth §§1154 ff. (attributive and predicate position); on the
larger questions of order, Dickey's Unit VI is the clearest modern statement.

## 3. The article

- **Whole classes and abstracts take the article**: οἱ θῆρες "wild beasts,"
  οἱ ἄνθρωποι "mankind," ἡ ἀνδρεία "courage" — where English has none.
- **The article makes nouns out of anything**: τὸ ἀληθές "truth," οἱ πάλαι
  "the men of old," and above all τό + infinitive, which nominalizes a whole
  action — τὸ τοῖς νόμοις πειθαρχεῖν, "obedience to the laws." Negate the
  articular infinitive with μή: τὸ μὴ ἀδικεῖν.
- **Article + participle is the standard Greek for an English relative
  clause**: οἱ λέγοντες "those who speak"; τιμῶμεν τοὺς στρατιώτας τοὺς ὑπὲρ
  τῆς πόλεως τεθνηκότας, "we honour the soldiers who have died for their
  city."
- **Predicate nouns drop the article**: βασιλεὺς ἐγένετο, "he became king."
  When both sides are arthrous the statement is a convertible identity; an
  anarthrous predicate is plain predication — ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν predicates
  love of God without converting.
- **The article as light possessive** where ownership is obvious: τὴν χεῖρα,
  "his hand."

**Drill:** N&H pp. 1–3. **Lookup:** Smyth §§1118 ff.

## 4. Aspect before tense

- **Outside the indicative, the tense-stems mark aspect only** — no time.
  Aorist = the bounded snapshot; present = the running film; perfect = the
  resulting state. So μὴ ποιήσῃς "don't do it" (once, at all) vs. μὴ ποίει
  "stop doing it"; παύσασθαι "to cease" (the event of stopping). Default to
  the aorist in infinitives, imperatives, subjunctives, and optatives unless
  you positively mean a process — the present is the marked choice.
- **In the indicative**, the imperfect covers ongoing, habitual, and attempted
  past action; ἄν + imperfect is a present counterfactual ("would now be");
  ἄν + aorist indicative a past counterfactual ("would have been").
- **The future participle expresses purpose**, especially with ὡς: ἦλθε
  λυσόμενος, "he came to ransom."

**Drill:** N&H "Tenses in the Indicative," pp. 10–11; "Tenses in the
Participles," pp. 12–15; "Use of Tenses in the Moods," pp. 92–93.
**Lookup:** Goodwin, *Moods and Tenses*, for anything the grid won't settle.

## 5. The participle engine

Greek subordinates with participles where English reaches for clauses; a good
English-to-Greek translation introduces participles the English never hinted
at. This is the single biggest step from correct Greek to idiomatic Greek.

- **Circumstantial senses**: time, cause, manner, condition, concession — the
  bare participle carries any of them, and context decides. Flavor when
  needed: ἅτε + participle for objective cause, ὡς for alleged cause or
  purpose, καίπερ for concession.
- **Genitive absolute only when its subject is not a constituent of the main
  clause.** If the participle's subject is also the main verb's subject (or
  object), use the agreeing participle instead: "having assumed this, he
  says" is ὑποθέμενος... φησί, nominative, never a genitive absolute.
- For **impersonal expressions** the absolute is accusative: ἐξόν "it being
  possible," δέον "it being necessary."
- **Supplementary participles** complete certain verbs: τυγχάνω (happen to),
  λανθάνω (escape notice), φθάνω (anticipate), παύομαι (cease: παύσασθαι
  φιλοσοφῶν), χαίρω, ἄρχομαι, φαίνομαι.
- **φαίνομαι discriminates by complement**: with a participle, "is
  manifestly" (φαίνεται σοφὸς ὤν — he evidently is); with an infinitive,
  "seems, but perhaps isn't" (φαίνεται σοφὸς εἶναι). The choice asserts or
  withholds your commitment.
- After verbs of **knowing and perceiving**, indirect discourse takes the
  participle: οἶδα αὐτὸν σοφὸν ὄντα, "I know that he is wise."

**Drill:** N&H "Genitive Absolute," pp. 34–37; "Participles," pp. 122–127;
"Impersonal Verbs — Accusative Absolute," pp. 128–129.
**Lookup:** Smyth §§2054 ff. (circumstantial), §2070 (genitive absolute),
§§2088 ff. (supplementary).

## 6. Mood machinery in independent clauses

- **Potential optative + ἄν**: might, could, would — the mood of the
  entertained thought. Three stock formulas run whole paragraphs of
  argumentative prose: δόξειεν ἄν τινι ("it might seem to someone..."), φαίη
  ἄν τις ("someone might say..." — the objection), ἔροιτο ἄν τις ("someone
  might ask..." — the question you then answer).
- **Hortatory subjunctive**: πράττωμεν, "let us act."
- **Deliberative subjunctive**: τί φῶμεν; "what are we to say?"
- **Prohibitions**: μή + present imperative to stop what is under way (μὴ
  ποίει); μή + aorist subjunctive against beginning (μὴ ποιήσῃς).
- **Wishes**: εἴθε or εἰ γάρ + optative for the attainable future; a past
  indicative, or ὤφελον + infinitive, for the unattainable.
- **The verbal adjective in -τέος** = "must be —ed": οὐ σκεπτέον, "it need
  not be examined"; the agent goes in the dative.
- **Impersonals take accusative + infinitive**: δεῖ, χρή, ἔξεστι, δοκεῖ.
  Note that δοκεῖ μοι with an accusative-infinitive clause stays singular no
  matter the number of the accusative subject — the impersonal parse.

**Drill:** N&H "Commands, Exhortations, Wishes," pp. 78–79; "Wishes in Present
and Past Tense," pp. 176–177; "Verbal Adjectives," pp. 130–132; summaries of
the subjunctive and optative, pp. 170–175.
**Lookup:** Smyth §1824 (potential optative), §1797 (hortatory), §1805
(deliberative), §1840 (prohibitions), §§1814 ff. (wishes), §2149 (verbals in
-τέος).

## 7. Conditions — the grid

| Type | Protasis (if-clause) | Apodosis |
|---|---|---|
| Simple fact | εἰ + indicative | indicative |
| Future more vivid | ἐάν + subjunctive | future indicative |
| Future less vivid | εἰ + optative | ἄν + optative |
| Present general | ἐάν + subjunctive | present indicative |
| Past general | εἰ + optative | imperfect |
| Present contrafactual | εἰ + imperfect | ἄν + imperfect |
| Past contrafactual | εἰ + aorist indicative | ἄν + aorist indicative |

- **ἄν never stands in the protasis** as a modal particle; its reflex there
  is the fusion ἐάν (= εἰ + ἄν) with the subjunctive.
- The future less vivid — "should...would" — is the argumentative workhorse:
  εἰ ἀφίοιεν... παράνομος ἂν εἴη, "if they should acquit on that condition,
  the verdict would be unlawful."
- **Mixed conditions are deliberate rhetoric**, not errors: εἰ ἀφίοιτε
  (optative) → πείσομαι (future indicative) — mist in the *if*, granite in
  the answer.
- **Relative and temporal clauses run the same grid**: ὃς ἄν + subjunctive
  for the general case (ἃ ἂν κελεύωσιν, "whatever they command"); the
  optative replaces it after past time.
- **Concession**: εἰ καί "even if" (conceding a fact), καὶ εἰ "even
  supposing" (conceding a hypothesis), καίπερ + participle.

**Drill:** N&H "Conditional Sentences," pp. 112–121; "Indefinite
Construction," pp. 148–149; "Temporal Clauses," pp. 150–159; conditionals in
oratio obliqua, pp. 178–185.
**Lookup:** Smyth's summary table of conditions, §2297.

## 8. Indirect discourse — three systems by head verb

1. **ὅτι/ὡς + finite verb** — after λέγω, ἀποκρίνομαι, and most verbs of
   saying. In secondary sequence the verb *may* shift to the **oblique
   optative**, which marks the content as reported-not-vouched-for; keeping
   the original mood keeps it vivid. This is a distancing dial, not a rule.
2. **Accusative + infinitive** — after φημί, νομίζω, οἴομαι, δοκῶ: δοκεῖ μοι
   συμφωνεῖν τὼ διαλόγω, "the two dialogues seem to me to agree." φημί in
   particular resists ὅτι in classical prose; give it the infinitive.
3. **Participle** — after verbs of knowing and perceiving: οἶδα, ὁρῶ, ἀκούω
   (§5 above).

- **Indirect questions** keep their interrogative, or take εἰ "whether";
  alternatives with πότερον... ἤ.
- **Fears**: μή + subjunctive/optative, "lest it happen"; μὴ οὐ, "lest it
  not." **Effort**: ὅπως + future indicative after verbs of striving.
- **Quotation mechanics**: φησί is postpositive — insert it after the first
  word or phrase of the quotation («πείσομαι», φησί, «μᾶλλον τῷ θεῷ ἢ
  ὑμῖν»); a forward-pointing οἵδε with raised dot introduces a quotation
  formally (λόγοι οἵδε· ...); and when excerpting, trim the source's own
  seam-connectives (an opening δέ) without guilt — they joined the original
  context, not yours.

**Drill:** N&H "Indirect Statement," pp. 48–55; "Indirect Questions,"
pp. 62–67; "Subordinate Clauses in Indirect Speech," pp. 84–86; "Verbs of
Fearing and Precaution," pp. 98–99; summary of the infinitive, pp. 186–189.
**Lookup:** Smyth §§2577 ff. (ὅτι/ὡς statements); §§2591 ff. (oratio obliqua).

## 9. Negatives

- **οὐ negates facts; μή negates everything non-factual**: conditions,
  purposes, generic statements, infinitives (except in indirect statement),
  conditional participles, wishes, prohibitions. Compounds follow the split —
  οὐκέτι/μηκέτι "no longer": ἐφ᾿ ᾧτε μηκέτι φιλοσοφεῖν, "on condition of no
  longer philosophizing," takes μή because the infinitive states a condition,
  not a fact.
- **Compound negatives reinforce** a preceding simple negative: οὐκ ἦν νόμος
  οὐδείς, "there was no law at all." A simple negative *after* a compound
  cancels it.
- **οὐ μή + aorist subjunctive is emphatic denial**: οὐ μὴ παύσωμαι, "I most
  certainly shall not stop" (Plato, Ap. 29d).
- **Verbs of hindering and denying take a pleonastic μή** (μὴ οὐ when the
  head verb is itself negated) — untranslated in English.

**Drill:** N&H "Negatives," pp. 80–81; the summary of οὐ and μή, pp. 162–165;
"Verbs of Preventing," pp. 160–161.
**Lookup:** Smyth §§2688 ff.; §2754 (οὐ μή).

## 10. Pronouns

- **αὐτός** three ways: oblique cases alone = plain "him, her, it"; ὁ αὐτός
  = "the same"; predicate position = intensive "himself" (αὐτὸς ὁ ἀνήρ).
- **Reflexives**: ἑαυτοῦ serves within its own clause. The **indirect
  reflexive** reaches back further — inside reported thought, it points to
  the person whose thought is being reported: ἀπολύουσιν αὑτόν, "they acquit
  *him*" — him the thinker, not themselves. (The pattern of Latin sē.)
- **Demonstratives divide the field**: ὅδε points forward ("the following"),
  οὗτος backward ("the aforesaid"), ἐκεῖνος to the distant or famous.
- **Relative attraction**: an accusative relative is attracted into the
  genitive or dative of its antecedent (ὧν for ἅ). But check the verb's own
  case-frame before diagnosing attraction — the "attracted" case is sometimes
  simply the case the verb governs.
- **Connective relatives open sentences**: διό, ἀνθ᾿ ὧν, ἐξ ὧν. Watch
  antecedent scope when a relative stands next to a plural noun it does not
  modify.

**Drill:** N&H "Pronouns," pp. 38–43 and 133–138.
**Lookup:** Smyth §§1204 ff. (αὐτός), §2522 (attraction).

## 11. From English to Greek — the art-form rules

- **Translate the thought, not the words.** English idiom must be dismantled
  to the fact meant, then rebuilt in Greek idiom: "the king ascended the
  throne" renders the accession, not the furniture. Greek has its own idioms;
  a good writer uses them — which is what the phrase-mining workflow in §13
  is for.
- **The dictionary discipline.** Never trust an English–Greek dictionary
  (Woodhouse or any other) as a *source*; treat it as a list of candidates,
  every one of which must be verified in LSJ before use — the full LSJ, fine
  print and all, to see the word's actual constructions and company. Dickey's
  version of the warning: "never use an unfamiliar word simply on the basis
  of what an English-to-Greek dictionary tells you: always look it up first
  in LSJ."
- **Stay inside attested classical prose vocabulary.** Poetic and
  post-classical words read as flaws in an Attic exercise. (For a deliberately
  Atticizing-patristic register this becomes a dial rather than a law — but
  you must know which words move it, which is the same LSJ discipline.)
- **Concretize abstractions.** Most English abstract nouns should become
  concrete constructions: "Alcibiades' plot was discovered" → "Alcibiades was
  discovered plotting" — a participle. Keep only those abstractions Greek
  itself uses in that sense; when you can't verify one, go concrete.
- **Build long sentences by subordination.** Greek prose likes extended
  periods with much subordination; combine short English sentences by
  subordinating, not by chaining with καί — and add participles freely.
- **Modern concepts**: transliterate proper names (the Septuagint and New
  Testament give the precedent: Ἀβραάμ, Δαυίδ), or periphrase the thing (a
  submarine: ναῦς ὑφ᾿ ὕδατι πλέουσα), or transpose to an ancient equivalent
  only where the context genuinely fits.

## 12. Idiom traps and small hardware

- English "is + abstract noun" → prefer a verb; ἔχω + adverb = "be in a
  state": καλῶς ἔχει, "it is well."
- Comparison: ἤ, or the genitive of comparison; ὡς/ὅτι + superlative = "as X
  as possible" (ὡς τάχιστα).
- μέλλω + future infinitive = "be about to."
- "On condition that" = ἐφ᾿ ᾧτε + infinitive.
- Result: ὥστε + infinitive for the natural result, + indicative for the
  actual one.
- Purpose, four ways: ἵνα/ὅπως/ὡς + subjunctive (optative after past time);
  the future participle; ὅστις + future indicative after expressions of
  choosing or sending.
- "The same as": ὁ αὐτὸς καί, or ὁ αὐτός + dative.
- **Enclitic accents**, the cheat-sheet: proparoxytone + enclitic gains a
  second acute (ἄνθρωποί τινες); paroxytone + disyllabic enclitic leaves the
  enclitic its accent (εἰκόνες εἰσί); an oxytone keeps its acute before an
  enclitic (ἑαυτόν τε) where it would take the grave before an ordinary word.
  Breathing and accent sit on the second vowel of a diphthong (αὑτόν,
  εἵλετο).

**Drill:** N&H "Prepositions," pp. 139–142, and the appendices: principal
parts pp. 223–229, compound verbs pp. 230–232, prose usages of the
prepositions pp. 233–237, accent rules pp. 240–245.
**Lookup:** Smyth §§181 ff. (enclitics).

## 13. Workflow — models, mining, analysis

1. **Compose from Greek models, not from English.** Before writing, find how
   a real author says the *kind* of thing you want to say, and take the
   construction with its case-frame attached. This is what Auden's *Greek
   Prose Phrase-Book* is for: idioms drawn from Thucydides, Xenophon, Plato,
   and Demosthenes, classified by topic — look up the subject, harvest the
   attested phrase, keep its syntax.
2. **Audit the skeleton first** when revising: every finite verb justifies
   its mood, every non-indicative form its aspect, every sentence-opening its
   connective. The connective check is the fastest win per second of any
   revision pass.
3. **Read the draft aloud.** The ear catches what the eye forgives —
   especially rhythm, hiatus, and a missing particle.
4. **Analyze what you admire.** Break any striking period from your reading
   into one-verb units, indent each subordination under its head, and name
   every unit's opening word and what it points to. Practiced on real authors,
   this is how the Atticizing writers themselves acquired the register:
   μίμησις of models, all the way down.
5. **Drill the weak construction.** When a revision keeps flagging the same
   construction, run the matching N&H exercises (each section's (A) and (B)
   sets are parallel — one for the first pass, one for revenge), and learn
   the special vocabularies; ignorance of common words is a constant source
   of weakness in composition.
